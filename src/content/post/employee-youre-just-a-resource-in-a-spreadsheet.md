Since mid-2022, layoffs have shifted from a "black swan" event to a constant background noise in the professional world. As we navigate 2026, we’ve hit a spike in job cuts that rivals the volatility of three years ago.

My LinkedIn feed is currently a graveyard of corporate loyalty. I see posts from people who gave 5, 10, or 15 years to a single firm, writing emotional "end of a chapter" essays filled with a sense of betrayal or deep grief.

Here is the hard truth: **we identify with our companies far too much.** We buy into the "culture," the "mission," and the "family" rhetoric, only to be blindsided when the math changes.

## The Illusion vs. The Spreadsheet

Corporate talk is designed to make you feel indispensable. They offer:

- **"We are a family"** — families don't usually fire you to improve quarterly margins.
- **Education budgets and free lunches** — retention tools, not gifts.
- **Vague promises of "growth"** — as long as that growth aligns with the ROI.

At the end of the day, you are an entry in a cost column. And that’s okay. Once you accept that employment is a transactional relationship rather than a personal identity, you actually regain your power.

## The "Dinosaur" Playbook: 3 Rules for Survival

After 15+ years in this business, I’ve boiled my survival strategy down to three non-negotiable rules:

### 1. Treat employment for what it is — a contract.

You are trading a specific set of skills and a specific amount of time for a specific amount of money. Do the work excellently, fulfill your side of the bargain, but never forget that the contract has an expiration date. When the money stops, the loyalty stops.

### 2. Always be looking.

You don’t look for a job when you need one; you look for a job when you *have* one. Keeping your resume updated and your network warm isn't "disloyal" — it’s career maintenance. The best time to see your market value is when you aren't under the pressure of a severance deadline.

### 3. Don’t take it personally; it’s just business.

When a company cuts 10% of its workforce, they aren't usually firing *you* — they are deleting a line item to satisfy shareholders or a board of directors. If you don't take the "family" talk seriously, you won't take the "termination" personally. It’s a math problem, not a character judgment.

## Final Thoughts

Stop waiting for a corporation to love you back. It’s a spreadsheet; it doesn't have a heart. Focus on building your own skills, your own brand, and your own financial security.

The goal isn't to be "un-fireable" — it's to be "un-shakeable" when the fire inevitably happens.
